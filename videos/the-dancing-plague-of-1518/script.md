# The Dancing Plague of 1518 — script (versão de ~5,5 min)


## [Introduction]
*[cartão: The Dancing Plague]*
*[cartão: Strasbourg, July 1518]*
On a hot July day in 1518, a woman named Frau Troffea stepped out of her house in Strasbourg, and began to dance.
There was no music. There was no celebration. Her husband begged her to stop. She didn't.
She danced until her feet were swollen and bloody. She collapsed, slept, woke up... and danced again. For days.
And then something far more frightening happened. Her neighbors started dancing too.
Within a week, more than thirty people were twisting and leaping through the streets. By August, some chroniclers claimed there were four hundred.
The city's response was so strange it almost sounds like a joke. They built a stage. They hired musicians. They paid strong men to hold the dancers up.
Today, you'll find the same chilling claim almost everywhere this story is told: that at its peak, the dancing plague killed fifteen people a day.
But when you go back to the records written in Strasbourg that summer, something doesn't add up. So what really happened in 1518? And why did hundreds of people dance until they could barely stand?

## [A City on the Edge]
To understand the dancers, you have to understand the city they lived in.
Strasbourg in 1518 was a free imperial city on the Rhine, crowned by one of the tallest cathedrals in Europe.
But its people were suffering. Harvests had failed for years. Bread prices soared, and families sold everything they owned just to eat.
Disease moved through the region in waves. Smallpox. Leprosy. And a terrifying new illness: syphilis.
To the people of the Rhineland, none of this was random. It was punishment from God. And one saint was feared above the rest.
Saint Vitus. It was believed he could curse a sinner with a compulsion to dance that would not end.

## [The Cure That Made It Worse]
As the dancing spread, the city council turned to its physicians.
Their verdict was not witchcraft, and not the stars. It was a natural disease, caused by overheated blood.
And the cure for overheated blood? The dancers would have to dance it out.
So the city cleared two guild halls, built a wooden stage near the horse market, and hired musicians to play day and night.
It did not work. The music drew crowds. And some in those crowds began to dance as well.
*[cartão: August 1518]*
By early August, the council reversed course. Public music and dancing were banned.
The dancers were taken by wagon to a shrine of Saint Vitus in the hills near Saverne, given small crosses and red shoes, and led around the altar.
And slowly, by early September, the dancing stopped.

## [Did They Really Die?]
So what about the famous claim? Fifteen dead, every single day?
The records written in Strasbourg that summer, the council's notes, the physicians' reports, the sermons, describe the dancing in detail.
What they do not clearly record is a body count. The figure of fifteen a day appears only in accounts written later.
Exhaustion, heart failure, or infected wounds may well have killed some of the dancers. But that famous statistic rests on far weaker ground than it seems.

## [What Really Happened]
One popular theory blames ergot, a fungus that grows on damp rye, and contains chemicals related to LSD.
But historian John Waller points out a problem. Ergot poisoning cuts off blood to the limbs. Its victims could barely walk, let alone dance for days.
His explanation is darker. A mass psychogenic illness, in a population pushed to its breaking point.
People who believed, completely, that Saint Vitus could curse them into dancing slipped into a trance, and did exactly what they feared.
The stage made it worse, because it put the dance on display. The shrine ended it, because it gave believers a ritual they trusted.
The dancing plague was not a poison, or a monster. It was what happens when fear spreads faster than food.
Hundreds of ordinary people danced until they collapsed. Not because something was done to them, but because their minds had nowhere left to go.
